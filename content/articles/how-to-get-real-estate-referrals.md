Referrals and repeat clients are the highest-converting, lowest-cost business a real estate agent can get — and most agents leave a fortune of it on the table. Learning **how to get real estate referrals** systematically, rather than hoping they trickle in, is one of the highest-leverage habits you can build. This guide covers the referral paradox, the stay-in-touch system that earns them, the scripts to ask, and the compliance rules you must respect.

## The Referral Paradox: They'd Recommend You — But They Don't

Here's the gap that defines the opportunity. Surveys consistently show the overwhelming majority of buyers and sellers say they'd use their agent again or recommend them — yet only a small fraction actually do. And referrals matter enormously: per NAR's member data, repeat clients and past-client referrals together make up a large share of the typical agent's business, rising to roughly half for the most experienced agents.

Why the gap between "would refer" and "actually refers"? It's almost never because clients are unhappy. It's because the agent went quiet after closing and the relationship faded. Fix that, and you unlock the best pipeline in the business.

## Referrals Are Earned, Not Just Asked

Before any script, understand the causal chain: *satisfied* clients rarely refer, but *engaged, delighted* clients refer readily. Referrals are the byproduct of an experience worth talking about — one where the client felt informed, supported, and impressed from first showing to closing.

## Stay Present After Closing: A Past-Client Cadence

The retention gap is caused by lack of follow-up, not lack of goodwill. Build a simple, repeatable cadence:

- **Touch base regularly** — a light, valuable touch monthly and at minimum a real conversation once a year. Neighborhood market updates give you a natural reason to reach out.
- **Anchor to milestones** — the home-purchase anniversary ("One year ago you closed on [address] — here's what your neighborhood's done since"), birthdays, and 1- and 5-year gifts (the five-year mark lines up with the typical move-up cycle).
- **Host one or two client-appreciation events a year** — low-pressure, relationship-first.
- **Systematize it in a CRM** — capture cell numbers, tag clients, and put the cadence on autopilot so no one falls through the cracks.

Keep this distinct from prospect follow-up: our [lead nurturing](/articles/real-estate-lead-nurturing/) guide is for people who haven't transacted yet; this is for the people who already closed with you.

## How and When to Ask for Referrals

Timing and framing make all the difference:

- **Set the expectation at listing:** "My goal is to give you such great service that you'll naturally introduce me to anyone you know who's buying or selling."
- **Ask two to four weeks after closing**, once things have settled but the experience is fresh — and again at any moment of visible delight (offer accepted, keys in hand).
- **Reframe "referral" as "introduction"** — it's clearer and lower-pressure.
- **Give an easy out and specific direction:** "No pressure at all — but if a coworker mentions they're thinking of selling, I'd be grateful if you passed along my name."

A simple text or email works: *"Hi [Name] — loved helping you find [address]! If anyone you know is thinking about a move, I'd be honored to help them too. No rush at all."* Make it effortless with a referral link, a branded email signature, and a "recommend a friend" page.

## Referral Etiquette and RESPA: What You Can and Can't Pay For

This is where good intentions create legal risk, so know the rules (and confirm with your broker — this isn't legal advice). Under **RESPA Section 8**, you generally **cannot give a "thing of value" to a non-licensee in exchange for referring settlement-service business.** And "thing of value" is broad — cash, gift cards (even a small one), event tickets, trips, and prize drawings can all count. That's why casual "refer-a-friend reward" programs are riskier than they look when the referrer isn't a licensed agent.

What *is* allowed: **referral fees between licensed real estate agents** (commonly around 25% of the commission), and simple, genuine gestures of gratitude within the rules. When in doubt, ask two questions: is anything of value changing hands beyond a bare referral, and to whom? The [CFPB's RESPA guidance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1024/14/) is the authority; your brokerage and state rules are the final word.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I ask for a referral without being pushy?

Frame it as an introduction, ask at a natural high point (after closing or a moment of delight), and give an easy out. Leading with genuine service rather than a hard ask keeps it comfortable.

### When is the best time to ask a past client for a referral?

Two to four weeks after closing is a sweet spot, plus any moment the client expresses delight. You can also re-ask during ongoing check-ins years later.

### How often should I stay in touch with past clients?

At least a light monthly touch and one real conversation a year, anchored by anniversaries, birthdays, and market updates so you're present without being salesy.

### Can I pay clients for referrals?

Generally not if they're not licensed — RESPA prohibits giving a thing of value to a non-licensee for referring settlement-service business. Referral fees between licensed agents are permitted. Confirm specifics with your broker.

### What's the difference between a referral and a review?

A referral is a personal introduction to someone ready to transact; a review is public social proof that helps future prospects trust you. Ask for both — they compound.
